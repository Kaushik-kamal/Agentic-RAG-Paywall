# Commercial Agreement Review

## Limitation of liability

The liability clause is where most commercial value is decided. A well-drafted cap
distinguishes **direct** from **indirect and consequential** loss, states a monetary
ceiling — commonly twelve months of fees — and carves out the matters that cannot or
should not be capped: death and personal injury, fraud, and often breach of
confidentiality and indemnified IP claims.

An unqualified exclusion of "consequential loss" is riskier than it looks. Courts
construe the phrase narrowly, and losses a party assumed were excluded — lost
profits arising directly from the breach — frequently are not.

Mutual caps are not automatically fair. A supplier's exposure is bounded by fees
received; a customer's exposure may be bounded by fees paid, which for a small
contract can leave a large operational risk unallocated.

## Indemnities

An indemnity is a promise to reimburse on an agreed trigger, and it operates
differently from a damages claim: it does not require proof of breach, is generally
not subject to the duty to mitigate, and may sit outside the liability cap unless
expressly brought within it.

The three questions for any indemnity are: what triggers it, is it capped, and who
controls the defence. An uncapped indemnity with no defence control is an open-ended
liability wearing the clothes of a narrow clause.

## Intellectual property and data

Distinguish **background IP** — what each party brings — from **foreground IP**
created under the agreement. Default ownership of foreground IP should be stated;
silence invites dispute. Where the supplier retains ownership, the customer needs a
licence broad enough to actually use the deliverable, including after termination.

For data, address ownership, permitted use, aggregation and de-identification
rights, and return or deletion on exit. A supplier's right to use customer data to
"improve the service" is a materially different bargain from a right to use it only
to provide the service.

## Term, termination and exit

Automatic renewal clauses need a notice period that a customer can realistically
meet. Termination for convenience is valuable but should be paired with a
proportionate charge where the supplier has invested up front.

Termination provisions without transition assistance are a trap. Exit terms should
specify duration, cost basis, data format, and the obligation to continue service
during the transition.

## Change control and service levels

A change control procedure prevents scope creep from becoming an argument. Service
levels are only meaningful with a defined measurement method, an agreed exclusion
list, a service credit regime, and a remedy — usually termination — for persistent
failure. Service credits are normally the sole financial remedy for missed levels,
which is a significant concession if not negotiated deliberately.
