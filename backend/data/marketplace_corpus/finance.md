# Valuation and Corporate Finance

## Time value of money

A cash flow is worth less the later it arrives. Discounting converts future cash
flows to present value at a rate reflecting their risk: `PV = CF / (1 + r)^t`. The
discount rate is the opportunity cost of capital — the return available on an
investment of comparable risk — not the firm's borrowing rate.

## Weighted average cost of capital

WACC blends the cost of equity and the after-tax cost of debt in proportion to their
market values:

`WACC = (E/V) · Re + (D/V) · Rd · (1 − Tc)`

Debt is cheaper because interest is tax-deductible and lenders rank ahead of equity
in liquidation. That does not make more debt automatically better: as leverage
rises, both equity holders and lenders demand more, and financial distress costs
grow.

The cost of equity is usually estimated with the capital asset pricing model:
`Re = Rf + β(Rm − Rf)`. Beta measures sensitivity to market movements; only this
undiversifiable risk is compensated, because diversifiable risk can be eliminated
for free.

## Discounted cash flow valuation

DCF projects free cash flow to the firm, discounts it at WACC, and adds a terminal
value. Free cash flow is operating profit after tax, plus depreciation, less capital
expenditure and less the increase in working capital.

Terminal value typically dominates — often 60–80% of total value — which is why the
perpetuity growth rate deserves more scrutiny than the explicit forecast. A growth
rate above long-run nominal GDP growth implies the firm eventually becomes the
entire economy, which is the most common error in practice.

## Relative valuation

Multiples — EV/EBITDA, P/E, EV/Sales — price an asset against comparable ones.
Enterprise-value multiples are capital-structure neutral and therefore comparable
across firms with different leverage; equity multiples are not. A multiple is only
as good as the comparable set, and differences in growth, margin, and risk must be
adjusted for rather than assumed away.

## Capital structure and payout

Modigliani–Miller shows that in a frictionless world capital structure is
irrelevant. Real-world value comes from the deviations: the interest tax shield adds
value, expected distress costs subtract it, and information asymmetry makes issuing
equity a negative signal. The pecking-order theory follows — firms prefer internal
funds, then debt, then equity.

Dividends and buybacks are economically equivalent before tax and signalling.
Buybacks are more flexible; dividends carry a stronger commitment and are cut only
under real stress, which is precisely what makes a cut so informative.
