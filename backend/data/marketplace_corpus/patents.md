# Patent Law and Prior Art Search

## Requirements for patentability

An invention must be **novel**, involve an **inventive step**, be capable of
**industrial application**, and fall within patentable subject matter. Novelty is
absolute in most jurisdictions: any disclosure anywhere in the world before the
filing date destroys it, including the inventor's own presentation or sale.

Inventive step — non-obviousness — asks whether the invention would have been
obvious to a person skilled in the art at the priority date, given the whole state
of the art. The assessment is objective and must resist hindsight, since every
invention looks obvious once explained.

## Priority and filing

The priority date is the first filing date; a later application in another country
claiming priority is treated as filed on that date for novelty purposes, provided it
is filed within twelve months. This is the Paris Convention priority period.

Applications publish eighteen months after the priority date. The gap means a search
of published art can never be complete for recent filings — unpublished applications
with earlier priority are still prior art against you.

## Claim construction

Claims define the monopoly; the description explains it. An **independent claim**
stands alone and defines the broadest scope. A **dependent claim** incorporates all
features of the claim it references and is therefore narrower — it survives if the
parent falls to prior art.

Infringement of a claim requires every element to be present. A product missing one
claimed element does not infringe that claim, however similar it otherwise appears.
The corollary is that broader claims are easier to infringe and easier to invalidate.

## Prior art searching

An effective search combines classification, keyword, and citation approaches.
Classification search — CPC or IPC codes — is language-independent and catches
documents whose terminology differs from yours. Keyword search catches recent
filings before they are fully classified. Citation search, both forward and
backward, exploits examiners' own prior linking.

Non-patent literature counts as prior art: conference papers, standards documents,
product manuals, and archived web pages. A search restricted to patent databases is
systematically incomplete.

## Freedom to operate versus patentability

These are different questions. **Patentability** asks whether you can obtain a
patent — it looks at all prior art, expired or not, anywhere. **Freedom to operate**
asks whether practising your invention infringes someone else's *in-force* rights in
a *specific* jurisdiction. An invention can be unpatentable yet perfectly free to
practise, and patentable yet blocked by an earlier dominant patent.
