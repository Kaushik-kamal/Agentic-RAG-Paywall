# Clinical Evidence and Trial Design

## Hierarchy of evidence

Evidence quality is conventionally ranked: systematic reviews and meta-analyses of
randomised controlled trials sit highest, followed by individual RCTs, then cohort
studies, case-control studies, case series, and finally expert opinion. The ranking
reflects susceptibility to bias, not certainty of truth — a well-conducted cohort
study can outweigh a small, poorly randomised trial.

## Randomised controlled trials

Randomisation distributes both known and unknown confounders across arms, which is
what no observational design can achieve. Allocation concealment prevents the
investigator from foreseeing assignment; blinding prevents knowledge of assignment
from influencing behaviour or assessment afterwards. The two are distinct, and
allocation concealment is the more consistently protective.

**Intention-to-treat** analysis keeps participants in their assigned arm regardless
of adherence. It preserves randomisation and gives the more conservative, realistic
estimate. Per-protocol analysis, which excludes non-adherers, reintroduces selection
bias and typically exaggerates effect.

## Interpreting effect sizes

Relative risk reduction is scale-free and persuasive but can mislead: a 50% relative
reduction of a 0.2% baseline risk is a 0.1 percentage-point absolute reduction.
**Absolute risk reduction** and its reciprocal, the **number needed to treat**, carry
the clinically meaningful information.

A confidence interval communicates precision. A 95% interval crossing the null is
conventionally called non-significant, but a wide interval indicates an
underpowered study rather than an absent effect. Absence of evidence is not evidence
of absence.

## Diagnostic test characteristics

**Sensitivity** is the proportion of true cases the test detects; **specificity** is
the proportion of non-cases it correctly clears. These are properties of the test.
**Positive and negative predictive values** depend on prevalence and therefore
change with the population tested — a highly specific test still produces mostly
false positives when screening a low-prevalence population.

Likelihood ratios combine both properties and are prevalence-independent, which is
why they translate cleanly into post-test probability via Bayes' theorem.

## Systematic bias

Selection bias arises when the study population differs systematically from the
target population. Attrition bias arises when dropout is related to outcome.
Publication bias arises because positive results are submitted and accepted more
readily — funnel plot asymmetry is the usual detection method. Registration of
protocols before enrolment is the principal structural remedy.
