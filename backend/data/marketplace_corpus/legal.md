# Contract Law Fundamentals

## Formation of a contract

A binding contract requires offer, acceptance, consideration, and an intention to
create legal relations. An offer is a definite proposal capable of acceptance; an
invitation to treat — a shop display, an advertisement, a tender request — is not.

Acceptance must mirror the offer. A response that varies any term is a counter-offer,
which destroys the original offer and cannot later be accepted. Under the postal
rule, acceptance by post takes effect when posted, not when received; the rule does
not extend to instantaneous communication such as email or messaging, where receipt
governs.

## Consideration

Consideration is something of value exchanged by each party. It must be sufficient
but need not be adequate — courts assess whether consideration exists, not whether
the bargain was wise. Past consideration is generally not good consideration, and
performance of an existing duty usually is not either, unless it confers a practical
benefit on the promisor.

## Terms and their classification

**Conditions** go to the root of the contract; breach entitles the innocent party to
terminate and claim damages. **Warranties** are subsidiary; breach sounds in damages
only. **Innominate terms** are classified after the fact by the seriousness of the
consequences of breach.

Implied terms arise by statute, by custom, or by necessity to give the contract
business efficacy. Courts imply a term only where it is so obvious it goes without
saying, not merely because it would be reasonable.

## Exclusion and limitation clauses

An exclusion clause must be incorporated before or at the time of contracting,
must cover the breach on its natural construction, and must survive statutory
control. Ambiguity is construed against the party relying on the clause — the
contra proferentem rule. Liability for death or personal injury caused by
negligence cannot be excluded.

## Discharge and remedies

A contract discharges by performance, agreement, frustration, or breach.
Frustration requires a supervening event that renders performance radically
different from what was undertaken; it is not available where the event was
foreseeable or self-induced.

Damages aim to place the innocent party in the position they would have occupied
had the contract been performed — the expectation measure. Losses must not be too
remote: they must arise naturally from the breach, or have been within the
contemplation of both parties when contracting. The innocent party must take
reasonable steps to mitigate.

Specific performance is discretionary and refused where damages are adequate, where
the contract requires continuous supervision, or where it would compel personal
service.
